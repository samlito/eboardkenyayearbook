### DAY 5 — Friday
**Type:** Education
**Topic:** Why conflict of interest management matters

**Post Copy:**

Conflict of interest is one of the most critical — and most poorly managed — aspects of board governance.

Here is why it matters:

A board member who has an undeclared interest in a matter before the board compromises the integrity of every decision made in that meeting. Not just the decision related to their interest — every decision, because the foundation of collective decision-making is trust.

Effective conflict of interest management requires three things:

1. A systematic process that prompts declarations at every meeting — not just once a year
2. Verification that the person making the declaration is who they say they are
3. A permanent, auditable record of every declaration made

Paper forms satisfy none of these fully. A verbal declaration around the table satisfies the first, partially, and none of the others.

This is a governance function that demands a digital solution.

### DAY 7 — Tuesday

**Topic:** The resolution tracking problem

**Post Copy:**

A pattern we see repeatedly:

A board convenes. Important decisions are made. Resolutions are carefully worded and recorded in the minutes.

Three months later, the board meets again. The Corporation Secretary presents the previous minutes. Several resolutions are noted. And then the question:

"What is the status of the resolution on [X]?"

Silence. Or: "Let me follow up and report back."

This is the resolution tracking gap. Boards make decisions, but without a systematic tracking mechanism, implementation falls to individual memory and goodwill.

The fix is straightforward: every resolution needs an assigned owner, a due date, and a status that is visible to leadership between meetings. Pending. In progress. Completed. Overdue.

When the CEO can see resolution status on a dashboard — not in a spreadsheet updated quarterly — accountability becomes structural, not personal.

Governance compliance is often treated as a burden. Something to satisfy auditors. A box-ticking exercise.

Governance compliance is not a burden. It is institutional armour.

### DAY 13 — Wednesday
**Type:** Education
**Topic:** The 10-day board notice requirement

**Post Copy:**

Board notice compliance is one of those governance requirements that seems simple but creates consistent challenges in practice.

The requirement: board members must receive notice of a meeting at least 10 days in advance (or as prescribed by the institution's governance framework).

The reality: when notices are sent manually — by email, by letter, by phone — tracking whether every member received notice within the required timeframe is difficult. Proving it to an auditor is harder.

Three things that make notice compliance reliable:

1. A system that calculates the notice period automatically and prevents non-compliant notices
2. An approval step — the CEO or authorised officer approves the notice before distribution
3. A delivery record — evidence that the notice was sent, when, and to whom

When these three elements are automated, notice compliance moves from "we try our best" to "the system ensures it."

### DAY 19 — Thursday
**Type:** Education
**Topic:** Digital signatures in governance

**Post Copy:**

Digital signatures serve a different purpose in board governance than in commercial transactions.

In board governance, a signature is evidence. Evidence that a person was present. Evidence that they declared their interests. Evidence that they approved a document. Evidence that they acknowledged a notice.

For this evidence to be credible, it must satisfy two conditions:

1. The signer must be verified — you need to know it was actually them
2. The signature must be preserved — it must be retrievable and tamper-evident

In E-Board, the conflict of interest declaration process combines both: the board member receives an OTP to verify their identity, then provides their digital signature using a signature pad. The declaration, the OTP verification, and the signature are all linked and stored.

This is not simply replacing ink with pixels. It is creating a more credible record than paper ever could.

**Type:** Thought Leadership
**Topic:** Why generic tools fail for board governance

**Post Copy:**

Google Drive can store documents. Microsoft Teams can host meetings. Excel can track tasks. Email can send notices.

So why do institutions need a dedicated board governance platform?

Because governance is not a collection of tasks. It is a regulated process with specific requirements:

- Papers must go through defined review and approval stages
- Notices must comply with advance notice requirements
- Conflict of interest must be declared, verified, and recorded at every meeting
- Decisions must be traceable from resolution to implementation
- Every action must be auditable with user, timestamp, and detail

Generic tools can do parts of this. But they cannot enforce the process. They cannot ensure that a paper does not reach the board without CEO review. They cannot verify a conflict of interest declaration with OTP. They cannot automatically maintain an audit trail with before-and-after values.

Governance requires a system designed around governance rules. Assembling one from generic tools creates gaps — and governance gaps are where institutional risk accumulates.
